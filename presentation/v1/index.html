Habit, Routine & Ritual

Habit

Automatic and unconscious, done without intention. Nothing breaks if it's skipped.

Routine

Deliberate but functional, built for efficiency, safety, or order. Skipping it breaks something practical.

Ritual

Consciously performed and symbolic, carrying emotional and social meaning. Skipping it just feels wrong.

Persona

Marie, 24
Professional from Seoul, working in Stockholm. Bachelor's in Economics.

"Moving to a new country to work and being away from my family is a big adventure for me, so during my pre-sleep routine I try to relax and wind down."

Background Story

Marie is from Seoul in South Korea and recently moved to Stockholm together with a roommate for her career. It is important to her to establish a routine to get used to her new environment and the new time zone as fast as possible. To stay in contact with her friends and family, she uses her phone a lot. Before she goes to sleep she usually calls her parents or answers text messages from her friends. She also likes to scroll through social media but wants to reduce her screen time, because she feels guilty about it, especially before going to sleep since it has an effect on her sleep. Social interaction is very important to her, that's why she likes to chat with her roommate every evening. To wind down before sleep she usually watches a movie or a TV show.

Problems
  • Using her phone too much and feeling guilty
Goals
  • Wanting to stay connected to home
  • Reducing her screen time
  • Relaxing and calming down during her sleep routine by consuming media
Behaviours
  • Consuming media before going to sleep
  • Calling friends and family, spending time with roommate

Three Directions to Take

Three of the eight themes carried enough tension to design against. Each one is a ritual already in place that the move has strained.

01
Heavy Phone Usage

The phone is both the line home and the thing that steals the night. The pull is real in both directions.

02
Consuming Media

A show is the one hour that belongs to her. The comfort is worth keeping; the open ending is not.

03
Social Interaction

Sharing the day is how she winds down, but the people she wants to share it with are asleep.

Direction · Heavy Phone Usage

Scenario

Marie brushes her teeth and cleans off her makeup after a stressful day. Looking at her phone she sees notifications from Instagram, Snapchat and a text from her best friend Chloe. She texts Chloe back and then calls her mom back home. After the usual greetings and “I miss you”s they talk about Marie's new apartment, work and how she's settling in, as well as how life is going back home in Seoul. They talk for about half an hour. Talking to her family makes her feel more connected to home. When they hang up she remembers the YouTube video Luke sent her earlier in the day. Her younger brother has a real knack for finding new channels about her favourite interests. She watches it and texts to let him know she liked it. She checks the latest posts on Instagram, sends a funny meme to Chloe, and then gets stuck scrolling the explore page. An hour later she is still on the couch, and when she checks the time it is already late. She instantly worries she will not get enough sleep, and feels guilty about scrolling despite knowing her sleep is worse when she looks at her phone right before bed.

Point of View

A young professional who recently moved across the globe uses her phone both to interact with family and friends and to consume social media. Her sleep is affected when she gets stuck scrolling right before bed and she feels guilty about it, but the interaction with family and friends is really important to her.

How Might We
  • How might we help people who have recently moved abroad reduce their screen time in the evening without losing out on precious interaction with family and friends?
  • How might we support synchronous and asynchronous social interaction with family and friends living far away without disturbing sleep patterns?

Direction · Consuming Media

Scenario

It is 22:45. Marie finishes washing up, an essential physical reset after an exhausting day navigating meetings, Swedish conversations, and unfamiliar public transit in her new city. The room is quiet, but the silence makes her head race with thoughts about tomorrow's tasks and the overwhelming impressions of settling abroad. To create a protective boundary around her night, she props up her tablet in bed and puts on a lighthearted show. This matters to her because it is the only hour of the day entirely reserved for herself, where she does not have to adapt or perform. She intends to watch just one twenty-minute episode to unwind, but auto-play rolls into another. The bright screen keeps her awake, and not wanting her me-time to end pulls her into late-night browsing. By 00:15 the comforting wind-down has turned into an unintended screen marathon, leaving her guilty, physically unrested, and frustrated that her sacred me-time derailed her sleep.

Point of View

A newly relocated professional navigating sensory overload needs a way to engage with gentle, bounded audio-visual media as a cognitive grounding anchor before bed, because she relies on external stories to quiet racing thoughts and reclaim personal autonomy. Yet open-ended digital screens turn that restorative ritual into passive, guilt-inducing sleep delay.

How Might We
  • How might we design a media wind-down experience that provides narrative comfort and cognitive distraction, while naturally tapering off to signal closure without abrupt cutoffs?
  • How might we transform bedtime media consumption from an addictive rabbit hole into an intentional closing ceremony for the day that still feels like me-time?

Direction · Social Interaction

Scenario

It is 22:30 on a Tuesday night in Marie's small room in Stockholm. She has just finished chatting with her roommate in the kitchen and is getting into bed after a long first week at her new job. This is the moment she most wants to call her parents in Seoul and tell them about her day, because talking it through helps her process everything that is new and lets her fall asleep feeling calmer. But first she has to do the maths: Seoul is seven hours ahead, and soon it will be eight, once Sweden turns its clocks back and she has to relearn it all over again. That means it is already 05:30 tomorrow at home, and her parents are still asleep. Earlier, when they were free in their evening, she was in the middle of her workday. She types a long message instead, then keeps scrolling in case they wake early and reply, which she already feels guilty about. She falls asleep without the conversation she needed, and the day she wanted to share goes untold.

Point of View

Marie, a young professional who recently moved from Seoul to Stockholm, needs to share her day with her family as part of her evening wind-down, because talking things through helps her process her new life and relax before sleep. Yet when she is ready to talk her family is asleep, and when they are free, she is busy.

How Might We
  • How might we help people who have moved abroad stay connected with friends and family spread across multiple time zones, as part of their bedtime routine?
  • How might we make it effortless to know when loved ones in different parts of the world are free to talk?
  • How might we help people keep in touch with a scattered circle of loved ones without having to line up everyone's schedules?
